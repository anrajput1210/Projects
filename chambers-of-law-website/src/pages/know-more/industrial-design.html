Industrial Design

How a product looks can be worth protecting just as much as how it works.

What qualifies as a design?

Under the Designs Act, 2000, a "design" means the features of shape, configuration, pattern, or ornamentation applied to an article, judged solely by the eye — it protects how a product looks, not how it functions or what it's made of. Common examples include product packaging shapes, furniture silhouettes, and surface patterns on textiles.

Requirements for registration

To be registrable, a design must be new or original, not previously published or used anywhere, and significantly distinguishable from known designs. Designs that are purely functional, or that are essentially a mechanical contrivance, generally fall outside protection and may need patent protection instead.

How long does protection last?

Design registration is initially valid for 10 years from the date of registration, and can be extended once for a further 5 years, for a maximum term of 15 years.

Design vs. patent vs. trademark

A design protects visual appearance; a patent protects how something works or is made; a trademark protects the brand identifiers used to sell it. A single product can sometimes carry all three — a distinctive shape (design), an underlying mechanism (patent), and a brand name (trademark).

This is a general overview of design protection. Whether a specific design qualifies for registration depends on a novelty assessment against existing designs.
