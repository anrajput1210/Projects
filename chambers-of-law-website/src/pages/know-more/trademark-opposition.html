Trademark Opposition

Whether you're opposing a conflicting filing or defending your own application, timing and evidence decide the outcome.

What is opposition?

Under Section 21 of the Trade Marks Act, 1999, any person may oppose a published trademark application within four months of its publication in the Trademark Journal. Common grounds include the mark being confusingly similar to an existing registration, lacking distinctiveness, or having been applied for in bad faith.

How the process works

  1. Notice of opposition (Form TM-O) — the opposing party sets out the grounds for opposing the mark.
  2. Counter-statement — the applicant has two months to respond, or the application is deemed abandoned.
  3. Evidence stages — both sides file evidence in support of their position, in sequence.
  4. Hearing — the Registrar hears both parties and issues a decision, which either party may appeal.

If your application is opposed

Being opposed doesn't mean your application will fail — many oppositions are resolved in the applicant's favour, particularly where the applicant can show genuine distinctiveness or prior use. What matters most is a well-evidenced counter-statement filed on time.

Opposition proceedings turn heavily on the specific facts and evidence involved. This page is a general procedural overview, not advice on a live opposition.
