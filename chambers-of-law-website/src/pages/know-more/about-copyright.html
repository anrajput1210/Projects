About Copyright

Copyright protects original creative work automatically — but registration still matters when you need to prove it.

What copyright protects

Copyright, under the Copyright Act, 1957, protects original literary, dramatic, musical, and artistic works, along with cinematograph films, sound recordings, and computer software. It protects the expression of an idea — the specific text, image, or code — not the underlying idea, fact, or concept itself.

Protection is automatic — registration is optional but useful

Unlike trademarks, copyright arises automatically the moment an original work is created and fixed in a tangible form; no registration is required to hold the right. Registering with the Copyright Office isn't mandatory, but it creates an official, dated public record that serves as strong prima facie evidence of ownership and creation date if the work is ever disputed or infringed.

How long does it last?

For most literary, dramatic, musical, and artistic works, copyright lasts for the author's lifetime plus 60 years. Different terms apply to works such as films, sound recordings, and government works.

The registration process

An application is filed with the Copyright Office along with copies of the work and supporting statements, and — where relevant — a "no objection" from any co-authors or prior rights holders. Registration typically takes several months, longer if objections are raised during the mandatory waiting period.

This is a general overview of copyright protection and is not advice on the copyright status of a specific work.
