Patents in India: A Growing Landscape

Filing volumes, examination speed, and policy support have all shifted meaningfully over the past decade.

For much of its history, India's patent system was viewed — fairly or not — as slow and under-used relative to the size of its economy. That picture has changed substantially over the last several years, driven by a mix of policy reform, institutional capacity-building, and a genuine shift in how Indian companies and inventors think about IP as a business asset rather than a formality.

Filing volumes are climbing

Domestic patent filing has grown steadily, with a rising share of applications now originating from Indian applicants rather than foreign entities filing into India — a meaningful shift from historical patterns where the majority of filings came from abroad. Sectors like software, clean energy, pharmaceuticals, and engineering have been particularly active, reflecting both India's growing R&D base and greater awareness among startups and MSMEs that patent protection is accessible to them, not just to large multinationals.

Policy support has expanded

The National IPR Policy, along with a series of procedural reforms, introduced measures aimed squarely at reducing friction for smaller applicants: significantly reduced filing fees for startups, small entities, and individual inventors; expedited examination options for startups and applicants in specific technology areas; and continued investment in Patent Office examiner capacity to reduce the historical backlog and shorten time-to-first-response.

Examination is faster than it used to be

Where a first examination report could once take several years to issue, expedited request mechanisms and added examiner capacity have brought that timeline down considerably for applicants who use them — making the patent system a more realistic tool for businesses that need clarity on their position within a normal product-development timeline, not just those able to wait out a multi-year queue.

What this means for innovators

The practical effect is that patenting in India is a more viable strategy today than it was a decade ago, particularly for startups and small enterprises that previously might have assumed the cost and timeline made it impractical. That said, patentability still turns on the same fundamentals — novelty, inventive step, and industrial applicability — and a growing, faster system doesn't change the value of getting the underlying application drafted well the first time.
