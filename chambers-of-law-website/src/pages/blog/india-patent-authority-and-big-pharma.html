India's Patent Authority and Big Pharma

Few areas of Indian IP law generate as much international attention as how the Patent Office treats pharmaceutical patents.

India occupies an unusual position in global pharmaceutical policy: home to one of the world's largest generic drug industries, and a patent system that has, at key moments, drawn a firm line against practices it views as extending pharmaceutical monopolies beyond what genuine innovation deserves. That tension — between rewarding innovation and preserving access to affordable medicine — shows up most clearly in two features of Indian patent law that regularly draw international attention.

Section 3(d) and "evergreening"

Section 3(d) of the Patents Act bars patents on new forms of a known substance unless the applicant can show the new form results in significantly enhanced efficacy — a provision specifically aimed at preventing "evergreening," where minor reformulations of an existing drug are used to extend patent protection well beyond the original invention's term. This provision was tested prominently in a widely reported Supreme Court case in which a major pharmaceutical company's patent application for a reformulated version of an existing cancer drug was rejected on these grounds — a decision that drew significant international commentary, both in support of India's approach to access to medicine and in criticism from parts of the pharmaceutical industry over its implications for innovation incentives.

Compulsory licensing

The Patents Act also permits the government or the Controller of Patents to grant a compulsory licence, allowing a third party to manufacture a patented invention without the patent holder's consent, under defined circumstances — most notably where the patented invention isn't reasonably available to the public at an affordable price. India's patent authority exercised this power in a closely watched case involving a patented cancer treatment, permitting a domestic generic manufacturer to produce a significantly lower-cost version. The decision remains one of the most-cited examples of compulsory licensing in modern pharmaceutical patent history.

The underlying tension

Pharmaceutical companies argue that strong, predictable patent protection is what funds the enormous cost of drug development in the first place, and that provisions like Section 3(d) and compulsory licensing create uncertainty that discourages investment in the Indian market. Public health advocates and generic manufacturers argue that unchecked patent protection in a country with India's healthcare needs would put life-saving treatment out of reach for a large share of the population. Indian patent policy has, so far, come down consistently on the side of guarding against extended or unwarranted monopolies — a stance that continues to shape how pharmaceutical patent applications are drafted and prosecuted in India.

Why this matters beyond pharma

Even outside the pharmaceutical sector, Section 3(d)'s efficacy standard is a useful reminder that Indian patent examiners scrutinise incremental improvements more closely than some other jurisdictions do — a point worth factoring into patent strategy for any industry built on iterative product development.
