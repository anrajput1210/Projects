Nestlé Wins Trademark Battle Over KitKat Bar

What the long-running four-finger shape dispute means for any business hoping to trademark the way a product looks.

Few trademark disputes have run as long, or travelled through as many courts, as the fight over whether the shape of Nestlé's four-finger KitKat bar can itself function as a trademark. The saga — playing out primarily in the United Kingdom and European Union over more than a decade, against objections from rival Mondelez (Cadbury) — has become a reference point for any business considering shape or packaging as a source of brand protection, rather than relying on a name or logo alone.

What was actually being fought over

Nestlé sought to register the shape of the four-finger bar itself as a trademark, separate from the KitKat name and logo. The core legal question courts kept returning to was distinctiveness: does the shape alone tell a consumer, without any words or packaging, that the product comes from Nestlé specifically — or would consumers simply recognise it as "a chocolate wafer bar" regardless of who made it? Evidence of long, heavy use and consumer recognition surveys featured heavily on both sides.

Why the answer kept changing

Different courts weighed the evidence differently, and rulings shifted the outcome across jurisdictions and over time — Nestlé prevailing in some rounds, and objections succeeding in others, particularly around whether distinctiveness had to be shown uniformly across an entire territory or region rather than in pockets of strong recognition. That inconsistency is itself the lesson: shape marks sit in one of the hardest corners of trademark law to win cleanly, precisely because the legal bar for proving a shape alone identifies a single source is high, and courts are cautious about letting trademark law block competitors from using a functional or common product shape.

What it means for other businesses

Very few products will ever accumulate the decades of exclusive use, marketing spend, and consumer-recognition evidence that a case like this turns on. For most businesses, the practical takeaway isn't "register your product's shape" — it's that distinctive packaging, colour combinations, and design details are usually protected more reliably and more cheaply through registered trademarks on labels/logos, industrial design registration for the visual appearance, and consistent, exclusive use over time that builds the kind of evidence a shape mark would eventually need anyway.

If a genuinely distinctive shape is core to how customers recognise your product, it's worth exploring — but going in with realistic expectations about the evidentiary bar, informed by exactly the kind of drawn-out dispute this case represents.
